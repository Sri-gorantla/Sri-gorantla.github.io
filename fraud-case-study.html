Case study

Fraud & Risk Analytics Pipeline

An end-to-end AWS pipeline for surfacing fraud-pattern signals from transaction data — the architecture decisions behind it, a bug I caught mid-project, and the detection tradeoff that shaped the final rule.

PythonAWS S3Glue / PySparkRedshiftAirflowPower BI

The problem

No systematic way to catch fraud before losses occur

A mid-sized retail bank processes tens of thousands of card transactions daily across online, POS, and ATM channels. Fraud review happens reactively — a customer complaint or chargeback triggers investigation, but there's no repeatable way to surface risk patterns before losses occur.

The question this project set out to answer: which transaction patterns most reliably indicate fraud risk, and how well would a rule built on those patterns actually perform — what does it catch, what does it miss?

Architecture decisions

What I chose, and what I rejected

Chosen

S3 raw zone, kept separate from cleaned data

Raw daily exports land untouched in S3, partitioned by date. Transformation logic can change and be rerun later without re-requesting data from source.

RejectedCleaning data at ingestion time (ETL) — this would destroy the ability to reprocess history if cleaning rules change.
Chosen

AWS Glue (PySpark), not pandas alone

Developed and proven locally before deploying to real AWS Glue — matches the tooling expected in production data engineering roles.

RejectedA pandas-only pipeline — technically simpler, but doesn't demonstrate distributed-processing skill, which was the point of building this.
Chosen

Star schema warehouse, proven in DuckDB first

Fact + 5 dimension tables, modeled for the grouping/filtering questions the project needed to answer. Proven locally in DuckDB before deploying to Redshift, since Redshift has a real running cost.

RejectedA single flat table — doesn't scale, duplicates descriptive data on every row, and skips dimensional modeling entirely.
Chosen

Idempotent loading — delete matching keys, then insert

Identified while reasoning through what happens if an Airflow task fails mid-run and gets retried. A plain INSERT would silently create duplicate fact rows on rerun.

RejectedPlain append-only insert — the obvious first approach, rejected once I worked through the retry scenario.

A bug I caught mid-project

When a "fraud signal" fired on 83% of transactions

My first geo-mismatch signal simply compared transaction country against the customer's home country. In testing, it fired on an alarming share of all transactions — a signal that fires on nearly everything discriminates nothing.

◆ Root cause

The synthetic data generator assigns merchant country randomly, regardless of customer home country — meaning most normal transactions already looked "foreign" by that definition. The signal wasn't measuring fraud risk, it was measuring noise in how I'd generated the test data.

The fix: redefine geo-mismatch to require a country change between two of the same customer's own consecutive transactions, within a 3-hour window — genuinely implausible travel, not just "shopped at a foreign merchant once."

The tradeoff

Catching more fraud always costs more false alarms

Six fraud-pattern signals were computed per transaction (velocity, geo-mismatch, amount anomaly, odd-hour activity, new-merchant exposure, card-not-present risk). A risk_score of how many signals fired simultaneously drove the final rule — and the threshold chosen was a real business tradeoff, not an arbitrary cutoff.

ThresholdDetection rateFalse positive rate
≥1 signal97.8%46.89% — unworkable, flags nearly half of genuine customers
≥2 signals — chosen85.5%1.87%
≥3 signals57.0%0.04%
≥4 signals14.5%0.00%

≥2 signals was chosen as the defensible tradeoff: catching 85.5% of real fraud while wrongly flagging only 1.87% of genuine customers. Threshold ≥1 catches slightly more fraud but at a false-positive cost that would be operationally unworkable for a review team.

Known limitation, reported honestly: geo-mismatch detection is the weakest-performing signal at 26.7%, partly because the injected fraud pattern in the synthetic data doesn't itself enforce rapid succession between transactions. A production version would need to either redesign the injected pattern or use a softer, distance-based geo signal over a longer window. Documented as a real gap, not hidden.
